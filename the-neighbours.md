# The Neighbours

**Outcome: you can say how PhotoPrism
differs from its best-known neighbour,
and from no software at all, so the
choice between them is a choice about
what you value.**

These are characterisations from the
projects' own documentation and from
this series' Immich guide — not
benchmarks. The lab ran PhotoPrism only.

## Immich: the other librarian

**Immich** is the neighbour readers
actually compare, and this series has a
guide to it. The two agree on the big
things: self-hosted, your photos on your
machine, a web and mobile interface,
search, maps, and face recognition done
locally.

They differ in where they start.
**Immich leads with the phone**: its
mobile apps back up the camera roll
automatically, and the library it builds
is largely its own — a managed store of
what the phones sent it, though it can
also watch external folders.
**PhotoPrism leads with the existing
folder**: a library you already have,
in folders you already chose, indexed in
place and never reorganised (chapter
1's promise), with its depth in
metadata, file formats and search over
what the files carry. Immich is a
multi-service stack — a server, a
machine-learning service, a database
and a queue — where PhotoPrism is one
program plus a database file. Neither
is "the advanced one"; they are the
same job imagined from opposite ends:
*collect from the phone* versus *make
sense of the archive*. Households pick
one, sometimes run both against the
same folders — PhotoPrism reading the
originals Immich collected.

## A plain folder tree: no software at all

The honest baseline. Folders named by
year and event, a file manager, and
your own memory for search. It costs
nothing, breaks never, and every tool
in this guide's world — PhotoPrism
included — treats it as the ground
truth underneath. What it cannot do is
answer questions: *every photo from
that lake*, *everything with a face*,
*what did we take in June 2024*. A
folder tree is storage, not an index.
PhotoPrism's whole offer is to keep
the tree exactly as it is and bolt the
index on top — which is why, if you
ever leave, you take the tree with you
unchanged and lose only the index.

## The cloud libraries, briefly

Google Photos and its peers remain the
zero-effort option: nothing to run,
excellent search, somebody else's
computer, somebody else's terms, and
an archive that is easy to fill and
tedious to extract in full fidelity.
This guide exists for people who made
that trade knowingly the other way;
chapter 1's framing — the same idea
with the cloud left out — is the whole
comparison.
